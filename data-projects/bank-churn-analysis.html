Bank Customer Churn Analysis (SQL)

MySQL SQL Customer Analytics

Analysis of a 10,000-record bank customer dataset to identify which segments drive churn, testing a set of plausible causes against the data rather than reporting churn as a single flat rate, with the goal of pointing the bank toward a targeted, rather than blanket, retention strategy.

Key Results

  • Age is the strongest churn driver in the dataset: churn climbs steadily with age and spikes to 56% in the 51–60 bracket, more than double the overall 20% rate.
  • Disengagement roughly doubles churn risk: inactive members churn at 27% vs. 14% for active members, a signal observable before a customer actually leaves.
  • One hypothesis ruled out entirely: credit card ownership showed no meaningful effect on churn (20% vs. 21%), meaning it's not a lever worth the bank's attention.

The Problem

The bank was losing one in five customers, a 20% churn rate across 10,000 accounts. On its own, that number doesn't tell the business much. Is churn spread evenly across the customer base, meaning it's simply a cost of doing business? Or is it concentrated in specific, identifiable groups, meaning it's a problem the bank can actually target and fix? Answering that question, using SQL to test it rather than guess at it, was the goal of this analysis.

What I Did

Before writing a single query, I set out plausible hypotheses a bank would reasonably suspect were driving churn: disengagement, demographic mismatch, and perceived account value. Each query below was built to test one of these directly, so every result either supports or rules out a specific hypothesis rather than being a standalone stat:

  • Established the baseline: total customers, total churned, and overall churn rate, to anchor every segment comparison that followed.
  • Tested the disengagement hypothesis by splitting churn rate between active and inactive members using a CASE statement.
  • Tested the demographic hypothesis by splitting churn rate by gender.
  • Tested the perceived-value hypothesis two ways: churn rate by credit card ownership, and churn rate bucketed by credit score range.
  • Tested the age hypothesis by bucketing customers into six age groups and comparing churn rate across them, the query that surfaced the strongest signal in the dataset.

Key Insights

Churn rises steadily with age and then spikes hard in the 51–60 bracket, where more than half of customers in that group have left. This is a larger gap than anything else this analysis surfaced, bigger than the gender split, bigger than the activity-status split, and the 41–50 group sitting right behind it at 34% suggests the pattern starts building before age 50, not just at it. Whatever is driving this, it's concentrated at the approach to and entry into retirement age, which points toward a life-stage product or service gap rather than a broad demographic issue.

2. Disengagement Roughly Doubles Churn Risk

Inactive members churn at 27% compared to 14% for active members. This confirms the disengagement hypothesis outright, and it's the most operationally useful finding in the analysis: activity status is visible before a customer churns, not after, which makes it usable as an early-warning signal rather than a postmortem statistic.

3. Gender Shows a Real but Smaller Gap

Women churn at 25% against 16% for men, a genuine 9-point difference, but a smaller effect than either age or engagement. The underlying cause isn't visible in transactional data like this, so this is presented as a finding that warrants qualitative follow-up (exit surveys, product feedback), not a pattern to act on directly without further investigation.

4. Credit Card Ownership: Hypothesis Ruled Out

Of card holders, 20% churned; of non-holders, 21% churned, functionally no difference. This is worth stating plainly rather than omitting: ruling out a plausible cause is as valuable as confirming one, since it stops the business from investing in a retention lever that wouldn't move the number.

5. Credit Score: An Extreme but Tiny Risk Pool

Customers with a credit score under 400 churned at 100%, but that bucket contains only 19 customers out of 10,000. Everyone in the 401–700 range sits in a tight 20–21% band, meaning credit score only becomes a meaningful signal at the very bottom of the range. Flagged here as a pool to monitor as it grows, not a headline finding on its own.

Step-by-Step Walkthrough

1. Baseline: customers, churned, non-churned, overall churn rate

View query 
-- Number of customers
SELECT COUNT(*)
FROM bank_churn;
-- the bank has 10000 customers

-- Churning (rate at which customers stop doing business with the bank or close their accounts) customers
SELECT SUM(Exited) AS Customers_Churned
FROM bank_churn;
-- the customers that have left the bank are numbered at 2037

-- The Non-churned customers
SELECT COUNT(*) AS Non_Churned_Customers
FROM bank_churn
WHERE Exited = 0;
-- the customers that have stayed with the bank and have active accounts are numbered at 7963 customers

-- Churn Rate
SELECT ROUND(SUM(Exited)/COUNT(*)*100,0) AS Churn_Rate
FROM bank_churn;
-- the churn rate is 20% at the bank

2. Churn rate by gender

View query 
SELECT Gender,
    COUNT(*) AS Total_Customers,
    SUM(Exited) AS Customers_Churned,
    ROUND(SUM(Exited)/COUNT(*)*100,0) AS Churn_Rate
FROM bank_churn
GROUP BY Gender;
-- females = 4543 total customers, 1139 churned customers with a churn rate of 25%
-- males = 5457 total customers with 898 customers churned at a rate of 16%

3. Churn rate by activity status

View query 
SELECT
    CASE
        WHEN IsActiveMember = 1 THEN 'Active'
        ELSE 'Inactive'
    END AS Activity_status,
    COUNT(*) AS Total_Customers,
    SUM(Exited) AS Customers_Churned,
    ROUND(SUM(Exited)/COUNT(*)*100,0) AS Churn_Rate
FROM bank_churn
GROUP BY Activity_status;
-- there are 5151 active total customers, with 735 customers churned at a rate of 14%
-- 4849 inactive customers, with 1302 churned customers at a rate of 27%

4. Churn rate by credit card ownership

View query 
SELECT
    CASE
        WHEN HasCrCard = 1 THEN 'Has'
        ELSE 'Doesnt Have'
    END AS Credit_card_status,
    COUNT(*) AS Total_Customers,
    SUM(Exited) AS Customers_Churned,
    ROUND(SUM(Exited)/COUNT(*)*100,0) AS Churn_Rate
FROM bank_churn
GROUP BY Credit_card_status;
-- there is a total number of 7055 customers that have a credit card, with 1424 churning at a rate of 20%
-- 2945 don't have credit cards, with 613 of them churned at a rate of 21%

5. Churn rate by age group

View query 
SELECT
    CASE
        WHEN Age BETWEEN 18 AND 20 THEN '18-20'
        WHEN Age BETWEEN 21 AND 30 THEN '21-30'
        WHEN Age BETWEEN 31 AND 40 THEN '31-40'
        WHEN Age BETWEEN 41 AND 50 THEN '41-50'
        WHEN Age BETWEEN 51 AND 60 THEN '51-60'
        ELSE '60+'
    END AS Age_Group,
    COUNT(*) AS Total_Customers,
    SUM(Exited) AS Customers_Churned,
    ROUND(SUM(Exited)/COUNT(*)*100,0) AS Churn_Rate
FROM bank_churn
GROUP BY Age_Group
ORDER BY Age_Group;

6. Churn rate by credit score bucket

View query 
SELECT
    CASE
        WHEN CreditScore BETWEEN 0 AND 400 THEN '0-400'
        WHEN CreditScore BETWEEN 401 AND 500 THEN '401-500'
        WHEN CreditScore BETWEEN 501 AND 600 THEN '501-600'
        WHEN CreditScore BETWEEN 601 AND 700 THEN '601-700'
        WHEN CreditScore BETWEEN 701 AND 800 THEN '701-800'
        ELSE '800+'
    END AS Credit_Score,
    COUNT(*) AS Total_Customers,
    SUM(Exited) AS Customers_Churned,
    ROUND(SUM(Exited)/COUNT(*)*100,0) AS Churn_Rate
FROM bank_churn
GROUP BY Credit_Score
ORDER BY Credit_Score;

Recommendations

Build an age-and-engagement risk flag: customers aged 41+ showing declining activity are the highest-probability churn segment in this dataset and are identifiable before they leave. The 51–60 spike specifically warrants direct investigation, whether a targeted survey or a review of what products this segment is or isn't receiving, since a 56% churn rate in one bracket likely reflects something specific rather than general drift. The gender gap should be treated as a research question requiring qualitative data, not a pattern to act on directly. Credit card status can be deprioritized as a retention lever entirely, and the sub-400 credit score group is worth monitoring as it grows, even though it's too small to act on today.
